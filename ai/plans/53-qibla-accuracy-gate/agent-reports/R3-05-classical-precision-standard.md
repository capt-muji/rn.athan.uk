# Round 3, report 5 — What accuracy did classical Muslim scholars themselves achieve and demand for the qibla?

Returned 2026-10-03. Sunni scholarship and history of Islamic science only; no Shia sources used.

Every claim is tier-labelled. **TIER A** = peer-reviewed academic scholarship. **TIER B** = primary
scientific/classical text in a named edition or translation. **TIER C** = museum/university/auction
catalogue data. **TIER D** = Wikipedia, blogs, popular articles, contemporary fatwa sites — used only
as a pointer, never as a final source. Unconfirmed items are marked **COULD NOT VERIFY** and listed
in §9.

## 0. The headline answer

The classical tradition ran on **two different standards at once**, and conflating them is the single
biggest error in this area.

| | Astronomers (`falakiyyūn`, `muwaqqits`) | Jurists (`fuqahā'`) |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of work | **arc-minutes** | **quadrants** (~90°) |
| Best attested precision | qibla tables correct to **±1–2 arcminutes** | "anywhere in your field of vision" |
| Instrument graduation | **1°**, marked every 5° (6 objects + a construction text) | — |
| What instruments *delivered* | **up to 11° off** on a tabulated entry; approximate formula ~10° off | — |
| Attitude to error | measured it, averaged it, labelled it `bi-taqrīb` | tolerated it, and validated the prayer |
| Criterion for "enough" | not stated numerically | **the limit of expert certainty**, and *ʿurf* |

So: a 14th-century muwaqqit would have regarded ±12° as **far outside his professional competence** —
he worked ~360× finer. The same muwaqqit's own legal tradition would have regarded ±12° as
**comfortably valid prayer**. Both are true, and the device question turns on which one you are
answering. §8 works this through.

**Three findings carry more weight than the rest:**

1. **§5.4 — the closest classical analogue to an uncertainty threshold, and it is juristic.**
   Al-Juwaynī defines the acceptable *jiha* as the span within which **a skilled observer cannot say
   with certainty that facing has been lost**. That is a confidence criterion, not an angle.
2. **§2.5 — a 1° graduation is what the makers aspired to READ, not what their instruments
   DELIVERED.** King's own catalogue entries record an 11° error on a single tabulated city and call
   one bowl's data "quite useless". The classical analogue of a fine display over an uncalibrated
   sensor.
3. **§4 — the Kaaba subtends 1–2 arcseconds from the medieval scientific centres.** Nobody outside
   Mecca has ever faced its body, and the jurists said as much: the tolerance "varies with nearness
   and farness".

## 1. The muwaqqits and the precision they actually worked to

**The profession.** From roughly the 13th century major mosques employed a `muwaqqit`, a professional
astronomer attached to a religious institution, whose duties included the prayer times and the qibla.
[TIER A — David A. King, *In Synchrony with the Heavens*, 2 vols., Brill 2004–05; Part V is on the
muezzin and muwaqqit in medieval Islamic society. King's own summary: "Closely associated with the
muezzin is the muwaqqit, the astronomer whose task it was to ascertain the qibla and the times of
prayer."]

**The precision — the key number in this whole report.** Shams al-Dīn al-Khalīlī (fl. Damascus,
mid-14th c.), muwaqqit at the Umayyad Mosque, compiled a universal qibla table. King:

> "The values are in degrees and minutes for each degree of latitude from 10° to 56° and for each
> degree of longitude difference from Mecca from 1° to 60°. Altogether there are over 4,000 such
> entries and these are mainly accurately computed **or in error by ±1 or ±2 minutes**."

[TIER A — King, caption to the al-Khalīlī qibla table (MS Paris BN ar. 2558), muslimheritage.com
feature authored by King; the underlying study is King, "Al-Khalīlī's Qibla Table", *Journal of Near
Eastern Studies* 34 (1975), 81–122.]

King states the same figure independently in his 1985 paper: "**Most of the entries are accurate or
in error by +1 or +2 minutes.**" [TIER A — King, "The Sacred Direction in Islam", *Interdisciplinary
Science Reviews* 10 (1985), 315–328, caption to Fig. 5.]

And in a review of *In Synchrony with the Heavens*: "Khalili's qibla values are remarkably accurate,
**usually good to one or two minutes of arc** (p. 694)." [TIER A — Imad-ad-Dean Ahmad, review,
*Archaeoastronomy: The Journal of Astronomy in Culture* XIX (2005), 103.]

**The table's size.** King gives the entry count as **2,880** in one account and "over 4,000" in
another, and describes the computation as still unexplained: "the entry in the table is the qibla in
degrees and minutes, given with such astounding accuracy that **we have not yet fathomed how the
2,880 entries in the table were computed**." [TIER A — King, "The Ottoman Mosque Fallacy",
muslimheritage.com, authored by King, 2018/19.] The discrepancy is King's own across two
publications.

**I can resolve it arithmetically.** King states the grid as latitudes 10°–56° and longitude
differences 1°–60°. Inclusive, that is 47 × 60 = **2,820**; a 48-value latitude band gives
48 × 60 = **2,880**. So **2,880 is consistent with the stated grid and "over 4,000" is not** — the
latter is likely a slip, or counts something else (possibly both of the two columns per latitude that
King describes, since "Each entry corresponds to two longitude arguments at an equal elongation from
the meridian of Mecca"). **Use 2,880.** The *precision* figure (±1–2′) is stated consistently in all
four sources above and is unaffected.

**This is the answer to "what precision did they work to": arc-minutes.** A table tabulated and
verified to ±1–2′ is a table whose compiler considered a 2-arcminute discrepancy worth noticing.
That is **0.033°** — roughly 1/360th of a 12° uncertainty.

**Did any treatise specify an acceptable error for aligning a mihrab?** **No — this is a sourced
negative.** See §5 and §6. The astronomers specified *their* precision implicitly, by tabulating to
the arc-minute; no text found sets a tolerance for the physical act of laying out a mihrab.

## 2. The qibla instruments and their graduations

### 2.1 The best-documented case: al-Ashraf's compass bowl (ṭāsa), Yemen, ca. 1290

This is the strongest result in the instruments section, because we have **the maker's own
construction instructions**, not just a surviving object.

Al-Ashraf ʿUmar ibn Yūsuf, third Rasulid sultan of the Yemen (ca. 1290), wrote a treatise on using a
magnetic compass bowl to find the qibla — with Ibn Simʿūn (Cairo, ca. 1300) these are "the earliest
known evidence attesting the use of the magnetic compass for the determination of the qibla."
[TIER A — Petra G. Schmidl, "Two Early Arabic Sources on the Magnetic Compass", *Journal of Arabic
and Islamic Studies* 1 (1996/97), 81–132.]

**The graduation, in the author's own words** [TIER B — al-Ashraf's treatise, Arabic text and
translation ed. Schmidl, from MS Cairo (Dār al-Kutub al-Miṣrīya), siglum C]:

> "then you divide the circle—which is called the degree scale (lit. circle of degrees)—which is next
> to the inner rim of the bowl **into three hundred and sixty equal parts, each of which is called a
> degree**. You divide the next two circles which follow this circle—namely, the circle of the five
> (degree) arguments (*dāʾirat al-aʿdād al-akhmās*)—**into seventy-two parts, each part of which
> consists of five degrees**"

And Schmidl's description of the accompanying manuscript diagram:

> "There are three circles... The middle circle is divided into **72 equal parts lettered every five
> degrees**. The innermost circle is divided into **360 parts, each degree marked with a short
> stroke**."

**So: graduated in whole degrees, labelled every 5°.** The working tolerance its maker accepted was
**1°**, with 5° as the labelling interval. The qibla values he feeds into it are whole degrees too —
he instructs the user to "count 27 on the rim of the bowl... that is to say, **twenty-seven degrees**.
The twenty-seventh degree is the qibla for the middle of the Yemen" [TIER B, same source]. The
diagram also marks *qiblat Taʿizz* and *qiblat ʿAdan* at 20° west and east of north respectively
[TIER A/B — Schmidl's reading of the diagram].

**Cross-check:** a 19th-century Ottoman/Syrian qibla directional plate is described by its catalogue
as "The inner circumference of the plate's rim is marked by units, **each of which is equivalent to
five degrees**, and also the four cardinal directions". [TIER C — Discover Islamic Art / Museum With
No Frontiers, object record ISL;sy;Mus01;38.] Same order of magnitude, 5° units, six centuries later.

1°.**

### 2.3 A CORRECTION: the British Museum's "every five degrees" is the SUNDIAL, not the qibla ring

My earlier draft listed BM **1921,0625.1** (ivory qibla indicator + horizontal sundial, **Bayrām ibn
Ilyās, 990 AH / 1582–83**) as a qibla scale graduated "every five degrees and subdivided into single
degrees". **That is a misattribution and it is now corrected.** Verified from the museum's own mirror
(islamicworld.britishmuseum.org/collection/RRM17193, same museum number and text as the cookie-walled
`W_` page):

- The "**labelled in Arabic numerals every five degrees and subdivided into single degrees and runs up
  to 105 degrees on either side**" scale belongs to the **horizontal sundial**.
- The **qibla ring** is something else entirely: "The outermost circle along the rim is **divided into
  72 sectors that give the names of cities and regions**." **No degree value is given for it.**
- And the curator's own comment on that ring: "**This is not an accurate 'map' but an approximation**,
  where places that are in the same area… are grouped together."

[TIER C — British Museum record 1921,0625.1.]

**This is worth more than the number I thought I had.** The most-cited qibla indicator in a major
collection carries a qibla ring its own curator calls an approximation, while the fine 1°/5° scale on
the same object serves the sundial. **The precise scale was for timekeeping; the qibla side was
sectoral.** A 5°-labelled scale lifted from this record and presented as a qibla tolerance would be
wrong twice over.

### 2.5 THE GRADUATION OVERSTATES THE REAL ACCURACY, and King says so bluntly

This is the most important qualification in the instruments section, and it cuts against the
flattering reading of §2.2.

**On the ʿAbd al-Aʾimma compass** — the one with the 1°/5° scale and 68 values in degrees and minutes
[TIER C — Sotheby's L07220, entry written by D. A. King]:

> "The values given on this instrument **do not always accord** with those on the other instruments
> or with the accurate values as given in the Timurid table. Generally the rule seems to be: **if the
> Persian instrument-makers could make a mistake, they would.**"

> "ʿAbd al-Aʾimma has **favoured localities whose qiblas round to multiples of 5°**."

And a specific error: **Medina is "some 11° off"** — entry 30 gives 26° against the table value of
37°10′.

**On the Damascus compass bowl** — the one graduated to 1° [TIER A — King, *In Synchrony*, vol. 2,
Pt XIVb]:

> "The geographical data on the bowl… are **so corrupt as to be barely intelligible**, and are, for
> any practical purpose, **quite useless**."

Note that King's own vol. 2 §9.2 describes the same bowl's data neutrally as "qibla-values to the
nearest degree", while his detailed entry in Pt XIVb calls them useless. **Both passages are King;
the detailed entry is the one to trust.**

**And on the approximate formula itself** [TIER A — King, *Sacred Geography & Qibla*, 2019]: the
formula favoured in al-Andalus "yielded a result **in error of some 10°** compared with the exact
formula applied to the same (inaccurate) pre-modern geographical data."

**So three distinct error layers stack, and the graduation is the smallest of them:**

| Layer | Magnitude | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scale graduation | **1°** | §2.2, six objects |
| Maker's tabulated values | **up to 11° off** on a single entry; some bowls "useless" | TIER A/C, King |
| Approximate formula vs exact | **~10°** in al-Andalus | TIER A, King |
| Medieval coordinate error (longitude) | **several degrees** | TIER A, Kennedy |

**A 1° graduation therefore tells us what the maker aspired to read, not what the instrument
delivered.** That distinction matters directly for the device question: it is the classical analogue
of a compass with a fine display and an uncalibrated sensor behind it.

**The arc-minute tabulation is real and it is the tradition's high-water mark** (Timurid table, to the

case; the ones King calls useless are the bad case. Both existed simultaneously.

### 2.7 The one hard number on tabulated qibla values on instruments

A late Iranian qibla-indicator, the *Jām-e Gītī-namā*, described in a treatise by Muḥammad Riḍā Yazdī
(fl. Yazd, ca. 1867). The paper confirms he derived tables from zījes and that his method is
equivalent to the standard approximate formula `q = arctan(sin ΔL / sin Δφ)`. [TIER A — Razie Sadat
Musavi, "Jām-e Gītī-namā: A Late Iranian Qibla-Indicator", *Tarikh-e Elm* 12(1) (2015–16), 17–40.]
Its qibla values are reported elsewhere as being given "in degrees and minutes and also in farsakhs"
— **that claim could not be confirmed from the paper's body text**; treat as COULD NOT VERIFY.

### 2.4 The Mecca-centred world-maps (the high point of the instrument tradition)

Three brass maps of the world centred on Mecca, Isfahan ca. 1700, with a cartographic grid preserving
*both* direction and distance to the centre. One is a circular brass plate 22.5 cm in diameter with
~150 localities named; the user reads the qibla off the circumferential scale and the distance in
farsakhs off a diametral rule. [TIER A — King, *World-Maps for Finding the Direction and Distance to
Mecca*, Brill/al-Furqān 1999; summarised by King in Rashed, ed., *Encyclopedia of the History of
Arabic Science*, vol. 1, ch. 4.]

King is explicit about where the error lives: "An approximation has been used on the world-map so
that the latitude curves are arcs of circles; **this produces slight inaccuracies noticeable only on
the edges of the map** (that is, in Andalusia and China)." [TIER A — same, Fig. 4.10 caption.]

**That sentence is itself an error budget.** The maker knew the approximation's error, knew where it
bit, and judged it acceptable in the interior and marginal at the rim. This is error-awareness
without a numeric tolerance. The circumferential scale's graduation interval: **COULD NOT VERIFY.**

A 15th-century Persian geographical table underlying these maps listed ~250 localities and
"**accurately computed** the qibla and distance from Mecca for each locality" [TIER A — King, ibid.].

**The maps' graduation is now verified from two independent auction catalogues** (§2.2): the azimuth
ring is divided into 360° with 10° increments, a 5° marker, and **punched dots at every degree**; the
cartographic grid is **2° × 2° cells**. Christie's claims "uniform and accurate distance and bearing
from Mecca for 149" localities, but that is a sale claim and refers the reader to King's *World-Maps*
Appendix C "for the degree of error" — **which was not obtained, so the maps' assessed accuracy
remains COULD NOT VERIFY.**

The essay review that was expected to restate the technical specifics does not: Emilie Savage-Smith's
review in the *Bulletin of the Scientific Instrument Society* 66 (2000), 32–35 was available only as a
preview and **gives no grid or accuracy figures** [TIER A, consulted, negative]. It does dispute
King's argument that earlier tables derived from lost maps, and it notes that **al-Khāzinī's
coordinates were "rounded to the nearest 10 minutes"** — which sharpens §4's observation that his
qibla values were computed to the arc-minute from coarser inputs.

### 2.5 Magnetic declination — the thing that wrecks the instrument's real accuracy

This matters more than the graduation. A compass graduated to 1° is worthless if its north is wrong
by 5°.

**Al-Ashraf (ca. 1290) almost certainly did not know about magnetic declination.** Schmidl's verdict:

> "The most likely conclusion would seem to be that **al-Ashraf did not know of magnetic variation**,
> even if he had an idea that the magnetic needle does not point to the north point every time."

[TIER A — Schmidl, ibid.] But — and this is the striking part — he hedged everything anyway. See §5.

**The Ottomans were probably unaware of the earlier Arabic compass literature:** "Ottoman astronomers
were probably unaware that the magnetic compass was described in Arabic texts in Egypt and the Yemen
ca. 1300" [TIER A — King, "Astronomical instrumentation in the medieval Islamic world", Brill].

**Declination WAS measured in the Islamic world, and the first value is now sourced.**

> **ʿIzz al-Dīn al-Wafāʾī, Cairo, 15th century** (ca. 1450) "determines a value for the magnetic
> variation **for the first time in the Islamic world**" — given by Dizer as **7 degrees east of
> north**.

[TIER A — Schmidl, *JAIS* 1 (1996/97), reporting Dizer, "The *Dāʾirat al-Muʿaddal* in the Kandilli
Observatory, and Some Remarks on the Earliest Recorded Islamic Values of the Magnetic Declination",
*Journal for the History of Arabic Science* 1 (1977), 257–262. Dizer's own paper was not obtained;
Schmidl and Barmore both cite it, so the 7°E figure is **Tier A at one remove**.]

**But it was known late and applied rarely.** King [TIER A — *In Synchrony*, vol. 1, Pt I, Fig. 1.7]:

> "Ottoman astronomers were **probably unaware** that the magnetic compass was described in Arabic
> texts… **although texts were available to them** on the first measurements of the deviation by
> ʿIzz al-Dīn al-Wafāʾī in Cairo ca. 1450."

**The earlier texts are genuinely ambiguous, and Schmidl refuses to over-read them.** On Ibn Simʿūn
(ca. 1300), who uses the phrase *inḥirāf dhālik al-maghnāṭīs ʿan al-khaṭṭ* ("the deviation of that
magnet from the line of midday"), she concludes such a deviation "**is not necessarily the result of
an observation of the magnetic declination**" [TIER A]. On al-Ashraf she says the text "raises
questions about [his] knowledge of the magnetic declination" and that the ambiguity "could be
explained by the inaccuracy of the observations and the deficiency of the magnetic compass used."

**A terminological trap worth recording:** Arabic *inḥirāf* means **both** the qibla angle **and**, in
Ibn Simʿūn's phrase, a magnet's deviation. Schmidl's glossary gives *mayl* as "deviation, declination
(astron.)" — **astronomical** declination, not magnetic. No Turkish or Persian term for magnetic
declination was found. So the vocabulary itself does not distinguish the two concepts, which is part
of why the texts read ambiguously.

**No source was found stating that any Ottoman or Safavid qibla-compass maker corrected for
declination.** One tantalising and unresolved case: King's note on the Ṣabbāgh compass (#8022) says
"the compass (**with a deviation of about 15°**) looks as if it is of European inspiration" — the
wording cannot be resolved into a built-in offset versus a reading error. **COULD NOT VERIFY.**

**The mosque-orientation evidence, and its author's own hedging** [TIER A — Barmore, "Turkish mosque
orientation and the secular variation of the magnetic declination", *Journal of Near Eastern Studies*
44 (1985), 81–98]. From 333 Turkish orientations Barmore argues the declination effect is detectable,
with a regression putting Istanbul's declination at zero around 1578. He presents it as a hypothesis
("if this thesis is confirmed"), calls the 15th–16th-century declination data "**poor indeed**", and
notes the 7°E value is "**the single point at 1450**" in his figure. **One data point carrying a
regression** — report the hypothesis, not a conclusion.

**Barmore's own view on achievable layout accuracy is worth quoting, with its status flagged** — it is
his opinion, not a measurement [TIER A, but an assertion]:

> "The ability to measure an angle and erect an aligned building within an accuracy of, say, **one or
> two degrees** are common in almost any age."

That independently corroborates King's repeated "a degree or two" (§4) from a different author and a
different evidence base.

**A modern qibla indicator is described by King as "including a magnetic compass and a list of
localities with the appropriate direction of prayer, mathematically computed and corrected for
magnetic declination"** [TIER A — King 1985, Fig. 6 caption]. The declination correction is the
modern addition; its absence is the medieval instrument's real error term.

## 3. Al-Bīrūnī: the achieved accuracy, and the uncertainty he himself owned

### 3.1 What he did

Al-Bīrūnī (fl. Ghazna, ca. 1025) "devoted an entire treatise to the determination of the qibla at
Ghazna... He used several different methods to measure the longitude difference between Mecca and
Ghazna, **took the average of the result** and then calculated the qibla by several different
accurate procedures. His treatise is **a classic of mathematical geography and scientific method**."
[TIER A — King, in Rashed, ed., *Encyclopedia of the History of Arabic Science*, vol. 1, ch. 4(a).]

### 3.2 His achieved accuracy — the Tier A figure

Kennedy, the standard authority on the *Taḥdīd*, describes the longitude work and then gives the
error:

> "Seeking the Δλ between Baghdad and Ghazna... his patron's capital, he made successive applications
> of his algorism for the stages through Rayy, Jurjāniya and Balkh. **Being rightly suspicious of his
> result**, he made additional calculations along a southern traverse through Shiraz and Zaranj,
> trying also a branch through Bust. **He accepted the arithmetic mean of the three findings** thus
> obtained. The final result **is in error by about a third of a degree out of twenty-four**, which,
> considering the crudeness of his original data, is very good."

[TIER A — E. S. Kennedy, "Mathematical Geography", in Roshdi Rashed, ed., *Encyclopedia of the
History of Arabic Science*, vol. 1, Routledge 1996, pp. 189–190; Kennedy citing his own
*A Commentary upon Bīrūnī's Kitāb Taḥdīd al-Amākin*, Beirut 1973, at p. 152 for the formula and
p. 164 for the eclipse observation.]

**So: Δλ error ≈ 0.33° (20 arcminutes) on a 24° baseline — a relative error of ~1.4%.**

Independently, Julio Samsó gives the figures: "He calculated the difference in longitude between
Baghdad and Ghazna as **24° 22'. The modern value is 24° 20'.**" [TIER A — Julio Samsó, "The First
Golden Age of Islamic Science", *Inference: International Review of Science*, translated from
Spanish.] That is an error of **2 arcminutes**, i.e. 0.033°.

**The two Tier A sources disagree by an order of magnitude** (Kennedy ~20′, Samsó 2′). Rather than
silently picking the flattering one: the likely explanation is that Kennedy is assessing the
*Taḥdīd*'s geodetic caravan-route computation, while Samsó may be quoting a different or later value
(al-Bīrūnī also treated this in the *Qānūn al-Masʿūdī*). **Which value corresponds to which work:
COULD NOT VERIFY.** Use Kennedy's ~0.33° as the conservative figure, since Kennedy wrote the standard
commentary.

### 3.3 The uncertainty he himself acknowledged

Two things are documented, both Tier A:

1. **He distrusted his own single-route result and cross-checked it by three independent traverses,
   then averaged.** Kennedy's phrase is "**being rightly suspicious of his result**" [TIER A —
   Kennedy, ibid.]. This is error-control behaviour: replicate, compare, average.
2. **He was explicit that conclusions are only as good as their data.** From the *Taḥdīd* itself:
   > "As the profession of astrology is based on weak foundations, as its subsidiary branches are
   > defective, **as the measurements made are inaccurate**, as the predictions made are based on
   > probability and not on certainty... so **their predictions can never be valid unless the data of
   > their object are accurate**."

   [TIER B — al-Bīrūnī, *Taḥdīd nihāyāt al-amākin*, quoted in Samsó, TIER A.] It is about astrology,
   but the epistemic principle — a result inherits its inputs' error — is stated plainly.

**A specific numeric uncertainty attached by al-Bīrūnī to his own Ghazna longitude: COULD NOT
VERIFY.** A self-stated error bar was searched for and not found in the accessible scholarship. What
is documented is the *method* of uncertainty control (triple traverse + mean), not a quoted ±figure.

### 3.4 His recommendation to non-mathematicians — and his contempt for folk methods

> "Every challenge calls for the right men... When (some people) were asked to determine the
> direction of the qibla they were perplexed, because the solution of this problem was beyond their
> scientific powers. You see that they have been discussing completely irrelevant phenomena, like the
> directions from which the winds blow, and the rising points of the lunar mansions... Of the
> majority of people (who write about the qibla in non-mathematical terms) **none are closer to the
> truth than those who use the Pole Star known as al-Judayy. By means of its fixed position the
> direction of a person travelling can be specified approximately.**"

[TIER B — al-Bīrūnī ca. 1025, quoted and translated by King, "Islamic sacred geography for finding
the qibla by the sun and stars: A survey of the historical sources", 2019, p. 19 — TIER A conduit.]

Note the structure, which matters for the device question: al-Bīrūnī holds a **high standard for
himself** (arc-minutes, triple cross-check) and simultaneously endorses an **explicitly approximate
method for the ordinary traveller** ("specified approximately"). He did not demand his own precision
of everyone.

### 3.5 Related precision data point

Al-Bīrūnī determined the latitude of Kabul at the end of 1018 as **34° 41'**, against a modern 34°
30' — an error of **11 arcminutes**. His instruments "in common with large instruments, [were]
divided into degrees and minutes", and when improvising he drew an arc "divided into degrees and arcs
of **ten minutes**." [TIER A — Samsó, ibid.] **The improvised instrument's resolution was 10
arcminutes.** That is his field tolerance when working with a scrap tablet and a plumb line.

## 4. The qibla tables: tabulated to the arc-minute

**To what precision were medieval qibla tables tabulated? Degrees *and minutes*, for the serious
ones; whole degrees for the practical lists.**

| Table | Precision | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| al-Khalīlī's universal qibla table (Damascus, 14th c.) | **degrees and minutes**; 2,880 entries; accurate to **±1–2′** | TIER A (King, *JNES* 34, 1975) |
| al-Ashraf's *al-jadwal al-ʿishrīnī* (20×20 = 400 entries, Yemen ca. 1290) | **whole degrees** of lat. and Δlong. 1°–20° | TIER A/B (Schmidl 1996/97) |
| Ottoman list of qibla-values, 90 cities | "qibla-directions **to the nearest degree** for 90 locations" | TIER A (King, "An Ottoman list of qibla-values for localities in the Ottoman Empire", 2018) |
| Instrument of 1518, 40 localities | "qibla-values **to the nearest degree**" | TIER A (King, Brill) |

| al-Khāzinī's geographical table | "coordinates are given to the nearest degree, **the qibla-values are computed to the nearest minute** but do not correspond happily to the coordinates" | TIER A (King, "The Sacred Geography of Islam") |

**Note that last row — it is the most interesting one.** A table computing qibla to the arc-minute
from coordinates given only to the whole degree is **over-precise relative to its inputs**, and King
noticed the mismatch ("do not correspond happily"). That is a Tier A historian catching a medieval
author reporting more precision than his data supported. The failure mode is recognised; it just
isn't theorised.

**The general tradition.** "Muslim astronomers... compiled a series of tables displaying the qibla
for each degree of latitude and longitude difference from Mecca"; "Some eight different tables are
known from the manuscript sources, and one, by Ibn al-Haytham, has not been identified yet."
[TIER A — King, in Rashed ed., vol. 1, ch. 4.]

**King's bottom line on achievable accuracy:** "One might think that any Turkish astronomer worth his
salt could have **calculated the qibla of his location to within a degree or two** if he had wanted."
[TIER A — King, "The Ottoman Mosque Fallacy".] He repeats the "a degree or two" formulation across at
least four publications (1985, 1995, 2017, 2018). **That phrase is King's considered summary of
competent medieval practice: 1–2°.**

### The absolute limit: what is the "exact" qibla even pointing at?

Computed independently; formula and inputs shown. Kaaba base is 11.03 m × 12.86 m [TIER D — widely
repeated; the Tier A survey would be Hawkins & King, "On the astronomical orientation of the Kaaba",
*JHA* 13 (1982), 102–109, which was not obtained, so **the exact dimensions are COULD NOT VERIFY**;
12 m is used as an order-of-magnitude target width]. Great-circle distance by the haversine formula,
R = 6371.0088 km; angular width `θ = 2·arctan((w/2)/d)`; lateral miss `= d · ε` (ε in radians).

| From | Distance to Mecca | Kaaba's angular width | Ground miss per 1° error | Ground miss per 12° error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ghazna (al-Bīrūnī) | 3,112 km | **0.8″** | 54 km | 652 km |
| Damascus (al-Khalīlī) | 1,389 km | **1.8″** | 24 km | 291 km |
| Cairo (Ibn Yūnus) | 1,287 km | **1.9″** | 23 km | 270 km |

**The Kaaba subtends about one to two arcseconds from the medieval scientific centres.** Even
al-Khalīlī's ±1–2 *arcminutes* — the finest achievement in the tradition — is ~60× too coarse to
"hit" the building. Hitting the physical Kaaba from Damascus requires ~0.0005°, which no pre-modern
and no phone-based method can deliver.

**This is the decisive structural fact.** *Nobody* outside Mecca has ever faced the Kaaba's actual
body. The entire tradition — astronomers included — is working with a direction that necessarily
misses the target edifice by tens or hundreds of kilometres. The fiqh in §5 and §6 is not a concession
to sloppiness; it is the only coherent position available, and the jurists said so explicitly (§5.4:
the criterion "varies with nearness and farness").

## 5. Did any classical scholar discuss instrument or determination error? — **Yes, in both literatures**

This was flagged as the key question. The answer is yes, twice over, from two different directions.

### 5.1 Al-Ashraf (ca. 1290) states an error term, its cause, and its magnitude relation

[TIER B — al-Ashraf's treatise on the compass bowl, Arabic ed. + trans. Schmidl; TIER A conduit]

> "**The essential thing in determining the direction of the qibla is to place the needle exactly over
> the center of the bowl** in such a way that the intersection of the needle with the rush-like stem
> is directly above the center of the bowl and **does not deviate from it, not even a little.
> Otherwise there might result an error in the direction of the qibla equivalent to this deviation
> (*mayl*).** The intersection of the needle and the rush-like stem... should be precisely over the
> center of the bowl **without any divergence (*zaygh*)**."

Read that carefully. He identifies:
- a **specific physical error source** (needle not centred),
- names it twice in Arabic (**مَيْل *mayl*** = tilt/deviation; **زَيْغ *zaygh*** = divergence/deflection),
- and states an **error-propagation rule**: the qibla error is *equivalent to* the centring deviation.

That is a qualitative error model in a 13th-century qibla instrument manual. **No numeric tolerance
is given** — he says "not even a little", not "within half a degree".

### 5.2 He systematically hedges the result with *bi-taqrīb* ("approximately")

> "the four (cardinal) directions are right **according to the best approximation** (*bi-aqrab
> taqrīb*, بأقرب تقريب), as we have shown"

and, on finding north:

> "This division opposite the head of the needle will then represent **approximately** the deviation
> (*inḥirāf*) of the meridian, which is the north point."

Schmidl's analysis of this habit is directly on point:

> "In his text on the determination of the north point **al-Ashraf always adds *bi-taqrīb* or
> *taqrīban*, that is, 'approximately', which can reflect magnetic variation or the insufficiency of
> the compass bowl or the observer's incompetence.**"

[TIER A — Schmidl, ibid.] And on the ambiguity of the reading:

> "**The problem could be explained by the inaccuracy of the observations and the deficiency of the
> magnetic compass used.**"

**So the answer to question 5 is: yes.** A classical author writing specifically about a qibla
instrument (i) flagged every instrument-derived direction as approximate, (ii) named at least one
concrete mechanical error source with its propagation rule, and (iii) left the magnitude unquantified.
Schmidl, a Tier A specialist, reads the hedging as covering exactly the three error classes a modern
engineer would list: **environmental (declination), instrumental (bowl deficiency), and operator
error**.

That is remarkably close to a modern uncertainty disclosure, minus the number.

### 5.3 The author's own epistemic humility

The treatise's closing [TIER B, same source]:

> "We have accomplished our task to the best of our ability and after investigating the matter with
> the leading scholars in this field... Whoever studies it, **let him not judge us too harshly. We
> are (only) one of those who have participated (in the search for knowledge) and not one of those
> who claim (to know much). We belong to those who have endeavored (*mujtahidūn*), not to the
> speculative debaters.**"

The Arabic is **ومجتهدون لا مناظرون** — "we are *mujtahidūn*, not debaters" — and the colophon
describes the work as determining the qibla **بحسب الاجتهاد والبحث** ("according to *ijtihād* and
investigation"). The instrument-maker explicitly frames his own work as *ijtihād*: diligent effort
whose result is honestly approximate and offered for correction.

**That is the classical frame for exactly the question being asked.** Diligent effort, openly
labelled approximate, is the standard — not certainty.

### 5.4 The jurists' answer: a test framed in terms of CERTAINTY, not degrees

The Arabic fiqh sources, searched separately, answer the uncertainty question too — in a form
startlingly close to what a device needs.

**Al-Juwaynī (d. 1085), quoted in al-Subkī's *Fatāwā*** [TIER B — al-Subkī, *Fatāwā*, Shamela
11498/149]. The *jiha* is the extent within which:

> **لا يقطع الماهر بالخروج به عن اسم الاستقبال**
> "**the skilled person cannot say with certainty** that facing has been lost."

**That is an uncertainty criterion, not an angle.** The tolerance is defined by the limit of what a
competent observer can *rule out* — the shape of a confidence interval. A Shāfiʿī uṣūlī of the first
rank framed the qibla tolerance as a function of the best available observer's residual doubt.

**Al-Rāfiʿī (d. 1226), same source** [TIER B]:

> **المتبع اسم الاستقبال وهو يختلف بالقرب والبعد**
> "What is followed is the name of 'facing', and it **varies with nearness and farness**."

So the criterion is explicitly **distance-dependent** — exactly what the arcsecond table in §4 shows
it must be. The jurists were not ignoring the geometry; they were tracking it.

**Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr makes the same point quantitatively in words** [TIER B — quoted in
*Tuḥfat al-Aḥwadhī*]: each region's permissible width is proportional to its position, and the
tolerance is tightest at the Sacred Mosque itself —
**وإنما تضيق القبلة كل الضيق على أهل المسجد الحرام** ("the qibla is narrow in the extreme only for
the people of the Sacred Mosque").

### 5.5 What is still a genuine negative

**No classical text specifies a numeric instrument tolerance, and none quantifies "slight"
deviation.** The jurists consistently refer the question to custom (*ʿurf*) or expert judgement
rather than a number:

- **Mālik** [TIER D — Dorar quoting *al-Tamhīd* 17/56]: slight deviation (**انحرافا يسيرا**) needs no
  repeat; severe deviation (**انحرافا شديدا**) is repeated within the time. **No degree is given.**
- **Hanbali *al-Rawḍ al-Murbiʿ*** [TIER D — quoted in an islamweb fatwa]:
  **لا يضر التيامن ولا التياسر اليسيران عرفا** — "slight inclining right or left does no harm,
  **by custom**." The standard is *ʿurf*, explicitly.
- **Ibn Taymiyya** [TIER D — via Dorar]: go by language and custom.

**The "45°" figure that circulates in contemporary fatwas was NOT traced to any classical source.**
It appears only in modern Tier D material. **Do not cite it as classical.** The nearest classical
approach to a number is al-Maqrīzī's quarter-circle (§6.1).

**So the astronomers modelled error qualitatively and hedged verbally; the jurists referred it to
custom and to expert doubt. Neither set a number.** That is the honest answer to question 5, and the
absence is consistent across both literatures.

## 6. The practical standard in mosque building, and the fiqh of tolerance

**There is no classical or Ottoman specification for mihrab layout tolerance.** This is a sourced
negative, and the scholarship actively explains *why* it does not exist.

### 6.1 The jurists' standard was a quadrant, not a tolerance

The 12th-century Egyptian legal scholar al-Dimyāṭī wrote what King judges the most significant legal
treatise on the subject — *Kitāb al-Tahdhīb fī maʿrifat dalāʾil al-qibla wa-naṣb al-maḥārīb* ("The
Book of instruction on the ways to find the qibla **and to set up prayer-niches**"), MS Oxford
Bodleian Marsh 592, copied 1196. [TIER A — King, "Islamic sacred geography", 2019, §7.]

Its doctrine:

> "The treatise on the legal aspects of facing the Kaaba by the 12th-century Egyptian legal scholar
> al-Dimyāṭī is the most detailed and the most sensible discussion of the subject by a legal scholar
> that I have come across... He discusses facing the Kaaba straight on or facing the general
> direction of the edifice, **stating that qibla within the quadrant about the former is
> acceptable**."

[TIER A — King, "The Ottoman Mosque Fallacy".] King adds that a critic "mock[ed] certain medieval
Muslim legal scholars who advocated an entire quadrant for acceptable qibla-directions, **which was
actually not a bad idea at the time**, and which would ensure that every mosque that [he] has
investigated was adequate, from the point of view of Islamic law."

King's own general statement of the two legal positions:

> "some legal scholars proposed that, whilst standing so that no one was actually facing the Kaʿba...
> was to be favoured, **it was also permissible to pray in any direction which would be within one's
> field of vision in that optimal position**... Since one's **field of vision is slightly more than
> one quadrant of the horizon**, both due west and due south were, at least to some, legally
> acceptable qibla directions for Central Asia."

[TIER A — King, in Rashed ed., vol. 1, ch. 4(a), with Plate 4.1 reproducing the two diagrams from
MS Oxford Bodleian Marsh 592, fols. 23v–24r.]

**The tolerance in the mihrab-building literature is therefore ~90°, derived from the human field of
vision, not from instrument capability.** The terminology is `ʿayn al-Kaʿba` (facing the Kaaba
head-on) vs `jihat al-Kaʿba` (facing its general direction).

Corroborating, independently:

> "The four sunni schools of law commonly accepted that there are two different kinds of Muslims:
> those who are in Mecca and can see the Kaʿba with their own eyes, and those who cannot. Those in
> Mecca had to be absolutely exact: they were required to pray towards the *samt*. But for the rest
> (that is, **ninety-nine per cent of Muslims**), **it was only necessary to know the *jiha*, the
> general direction.** ... For some *fuqahāʾ*, **the *jiha* was an angle of ninety degrees**:
> wherever Muslims prayed inside this angle was the *jiha*. Other scholars, however, thought that it
> was **legitimate to pray within a margin of 180 degrees.**"

[TIER A — Mónica Rius, "Finding the Sacred Direction: Medieval Books on the Qibla", in *Cosmology
Across Cultures*, ASP Conference Series 409 (2009), p. 178; she cites her own *La alquibla en
al-Andalus y al-Magrib al-Aqsà*, Barcelona 2000.]

**Attributing the 90° and 180° views to named jurists: COULD NOT VERIFY, but with two strong
candidates found.**

- **Nearest to 90°: al-Maqrīzī, *al-Khiṭaṭ*** [TIER B — one page, Beirut ed. p. 34, retrieved
  mid-sentence]. The *jiha* "**يتسع ببعد المدى ويضيق بقربه، فأقصى ما ينتهي إليه اتساعه ربع دائرة
  الأفق**" — "widens with distance and narrows with nearness, and **the utmost extent of its widening
  is a quarter of the horizon's circle**." The `ʿayn` line runs through the middle of that quarter, so
  "45° either side" is arithmetic on his words, not his words. He is a historian; his madhhab is not
  stated in the retrieved page.
- **Nearest to 180°: Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, via Ibn Rajab's *Fatḥ al-Bārī*** [TIER B]: a prayer anywhere
  between east and west is valid, though the middle is preferable —
  "**وكان إلى أحد الشقين أميل فصلاته تامة، إذا كان بين المشرق والمغرب**". But this is **not a general
  180° rule**: Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr explains it holds for a place due north of Mecca (Medina's
  east–west), reversing for Yemen and running south–north for Iraq and Khurasan.

**Pairing Maqrīzī with Rius's 90° and Aḥmad with her 180° is inference. No source makes that
pairing.** Rius's own 2000 monograph (indexed at pp. 89–91 under *jiha kubrā* / *jiha ṣughrā*) would
likely settle it and was not obtained.

### 6.2 The hadith that underwrites the wide tolerance — with its grading

> **ما بين المشرق والمغرب قبلة**
> "What is between the east and the west is a qibla."

[TIER A conduit — King quotes it as an epigraph in "The Ottoman Mosque Fallacy" and in Rashed ed.,
vol. 1, ch. 4, noting the Prophet said it in Medina.]

**Collections and chains** [TIER B — the collections' own texts]:

- **Tirmidhī no. 342**, from Abū Hurayra. The first chain runs through Abū Maʿshar Najīḥ al-Sindī,
  who is weak; Tirmidhī records that al-Bukhārī would not narrate from him (**قال محمد لا أروي عنه شيئا**).
- **Second chain:** al-Makhramī ← ʿUthmān b. Muḥammad al-Akhnasī ← Saʿīd al-Maqburī ← Abū Hurayra.
  Tirmidhī grades it **حسن صحيح**; al-Bukhārī calls it "**أقوى من حديث أبي معشر وأصح**".
- Also narrated by **Ibn Mājah** (no. 1011), **al-Ḥākim** and **al-Dāraquṭnī**.

**Grading is contested, and that matters:**

- **Al-Albānī**, *Irwāʾ al-Ghalīl* no. 292 [TIER B]: **ṣaḥīḥ**.
- **Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal**, via Ibn Rajab's *Fatḥ al-Bārī* [TIER B]: "**ليس له إسناد**" and once
  "**ليس بالقوي**". For Aḥmad the sound version is **ʿUmar's** saying, which he graded
  **صحيح عن عمر**: "**إذا جعلت المغرب عن يمينك والمشرق عن يسارك فما بينهما قبلة**" — "if you put the
  west on your right and the east on your left, what is between them is a qibla."
- **Ibn Rajab** rejects al-Ḥākim's ṣaḥīḥ grade of the marfūʿ Ibn ʿUmar route: "**وليس كما قال**",
  citing al-Dāraquṭnī and Abū Zurʿa.

**How jurists used it as a tolerance basis** [all TIER B unless noted]:

- **Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr**, quoted in *Tuḥfat al-Aḥwadhī*: the width is **regional and proportional**, and
  he calls the Medina-specific reading "**صحيح لا مدفع له ولا خلاف بين أهل العلم فيه**". The same
  passage reports al-ʿIrāqī, al-Bayhaqī (*al-Khilāfiyyāt*) and al-Suyūṭī reading it as addressed to
  Medina and places aligned with it.
- **Ibn Rushd**, *Bidāyat al-Mujtahid*: reports the hadith as the basis for the *jiha* view and adds
  the practical argument that requiring the exact `ʿayn` would be **حرجا** (an undue burden).
- **Companion sayings in the same vein** — ʿUthmān: "**كيف يخطئ الرجل الصلاة وما بين المشرق والمغرب
  قبلة ما لم يتحيز المشرق عمدا**"; similar attributed to ʿAlī, Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn ʿAbbās, Saʿīd b. Jubayr,
  Mujāhid and al-Nakhaʿī.

**So the 180° reading rests on a report whose marfūʿ status Aḥmad and Ibn Rajab doubted, and whose
regional application Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr restricted.** The ~90° quadrant has the firmer footing.

### 6.3 The four schools on a prayer performed after ijtihād that proves mistaken

Discovered **after** the prayer:

| School | Ruling | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Hanbali** | **Valid, no repeat** — "**لم يلزمه الإعادة**" | Ibn Qudāma, *al-Mughnī* no. 628 [TIER B]. He reports Mālik, Abū Ḥanīfa and one Shāfiʿī *qawl* agreeing. |
| **Maliki** | **Valid**; repeating within the time is **recommended** — "**إلا أن مالكا استحب له الإعادة في الوقت**" | Ibn Rushd, *Bidāyat al-Mujtahid* [TIER B] |
| **Shafiʿi** | **Two reports**: the *Umm* says repeat, the *Qadīm* says no | *al-Majmūʿ* 3/222 [TIER D — via Dorar]; the Kuwaiti Encyclopedia calls repeat the *aẓhar* |
| **Hanafi** | **Valid** | *al-Hidāya* 1/47, *Fatḥ al-Qadīr* 1/272 [TIER D — via Dorar only; originals not read] |

**The scriptural basis for validity** [TIER B — *al-Mughnī*]: the ʿĀmir b. Rabīʿa report (Tirmidhī,
Ibn Mājah) and Jābir's report (al-Dāraquṭnī, al-Ḥākim, al-Bayhaqī). Albānī (*Irwāʾ* no. 291) grades
ʿĀmir's *ḥasan* and raises Jābir's to *ḥasan* by combining routes; **Ibn Qudāma and al-ʿUqaylī call
Jābir's weak.** Ibn Rushd notes most hold Q2:115 abrogated by Q2:144.

**One qualifier worth noting** [TIER D — Kuwaiti Encyclopedia]: Hanafi and Maliki authorities hold
that if the qibla *signs were obvious*, he must repeat. **Ignorance of fine astronomy is excused;
ignorance of the sun and moon is not.** That is a competence floor rather than an accuracy threshold,
and it is the closest the fiqh comes to "you should have used the available instrument".

### 6.4 The evidence hierarchy

**No single classical text states a four-step ladder** (certainty > report > ijtihād > taqlīd). The
pieces are school-specific [TIER D — Kuwaiti Encyclopedia ¶21–25, footnoted to *Nihāyat al-Muḥtāj*,
*Radd al-Muḥtār*, *al-Mughnī*]:

- **Shāfiʿī**, when evidences conflict: a *mutawātir* report → an eyewitness report of seeing the
  Kaaba → **recognised mihrabs** → the pole star.
- **Hanbali:** the report of someone who knows for certain precedes ijtihād.
- **Hanafi:** the stars in open country first, then asking someone who knows, then *taḥarrī*
  (best-effort) — which is **not** the same as the Shāfiʿī *ijtihād*.
- **Mihrabs stand in for a report:** "**جرى ذلك مجرى الخبر**".
- **Taqlīd:** a *mujtahid* may not do taqlīd; if he does, his prayer is invalid **even if he hits the
  qibla** [TIER B — al-Nawawī, *al-Majmūʿ* 3/203–4]. And a *muqallid* means anyone who does not know
  the evidences, **even a jurist** [TIER B — Ibn Qudāma, *al-Mughnī*].

**That last pair is the sharpest thing in the fiqh for a device.** Al-Nawawī's rule makes the
*process* decisive and the *outcome* insufficient: accidentally facing correctly without exercising
the competence you possess is still a failure. The mirror-image is the point — **exercising the
competence and being somewhat wrong is a success.** Which is §6.3's unanimous ruling.

### 6.5 Mosques were frequently not laid out by astronomers at all

> "it is quite apparent from the orientations of mosques erected between the seventh and the
> nineteenth centuries that **the astronomers were not always consulted on the matter of the qibla**.
> Some mosques, to be sure, are indeed oriented in the qiblas determined by the astronomers... but
> **they constitute a minority**."

[TIER A — King, in Rashed ed., vol. 1, ch. 4(a).] And: "**those who laid out mosques were not always
in touch with the astronomers**; rather, they would use astronomical alignments or qiblas derived
from diagrams of sacred geography or traditional methods popular in the region or the standard
approximate geometrical procedure" [TIER A — King, "The Ottoman Mosque Fallacy"].

So the arc-minute precision of §1 and §4 was **available but not generally applied** to buildings.

### 6.6 Ottoman / Sinan specifically

**No documented qibla-setting procedure with a stated tolerance for Ottoman imperial architecture was
found.** What is documented:

- The Ottomans had a calculated list of qibla-values for 90 cities, to the nearest degree, which
  King found in the Bibliothèque nationale de France — and which "**correspond to some of the mosque
  orientations**" that a critic had misread as not facing Mecca. [TIER A — King, "An Ottoman list of
  qibla-values…", 2018, and "The Ottoman Mosque Fallacy".]
- Ottoman astronomers' standard textbooks were al-Jaghmīnī's *al-Mulakhkhaṣ fī ʾl-hayʾa* and
  al-Ṭūsī's *al-Tadhkira*, "each [of which] proposed an **approximate method** for finding the qibla
  that was obviously less accurate than the exact method... **The divergence between the results
  using these two methods and MODERN geographical data for Western Anatolia might be a few
  degrees**." [TIER A — King, ibid.]
- Astrologers, not astronomers, were the ones reliably present at a foundation: "They were always
  present to choose an auspicious day for laying the foundations of a new mosque." [TIER A — ibid.]
- Ottoman latitude values for their own capital "varied between 41° and 48°", though most
  15th-century values were "more appropriately in the range 41°–42°" [TIER A — ibid.]. **A 1°
  latitude error moves the qibla by about 1.8° at Istanbul's position** (§10 sensitivity).

### 6.7 What Ottoman mosques actually achieved — independently measured

| Study | Sample | Measured deviation from true qibla | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ilçi, Ozulu et al. 2018 | 8 mosques, Çorum, Turkey, 14th–21st c. | **0°00′39″ to 17°41′31″**; recent mosques more accurate, earlier more deviant; all judged "within acceptable boundaries" | TIER A (*Tehnički vjesnik* 25(6), 2018) |

**Ottoman-era mosques sit in the 6–18° band.** That is the measured historical practice of the most
mathematically sophisticated Islamic polity, in its own heartland.

The Ilçi et al. framing of the legal requirement, as working surveyors state it:

> "Facing towards the qibla... is one of the six conditions or requisites of the prayer for being

> acceptable declination, his/her prayer is invalid** according to scholarly consensus."

[TIER A — Ilçi, Ozulu et al. 2018.] They assert an "acceptable declination" exists — **but do not give

## 7. The critical methodological warning: mathematical vs folk qibla

The brief asked to distinguish these, and the entire Tier A literature insists on it. Getting this
wrong manufactures false deviation figures.

**There are at least three different "correct" qiblas for any medieval locality:**

1. **The folk-astronomy qibla** — cardinal and solstitial directions, risings/settings of qibla-stars
   (notably Canopus/Suhayl), wind directions. Used from the 7th to the 21st century, "invariably [by]
   scholars of the sacred law in addition to being knowledgeable in folk astronomy." The rationale is
   not sloppiness: **the Kaaba itself is astronomically aligned** (major axis to the rising of
   Canopus, minor axis to summer sunrise / winter sunset), so facing an astronomical direction was a
   way of facing a *wall of the edifice*. [TIER A — King, passim; Hawkins & King, *JHA* 13 (1982).]
2. **The medieval mathematical qibla** — computed by exact or approximate formula from *medieval*
   coordinates. Correct mathematics, inaccurate inputs.
3. **The modern qibla** — modern coordinates, exact formula.

King's warning, repeated verbatim across publications:

> "**Modern values of directions from one place to a distant other one are irrelevant to
> investigating the orientations of historical edifices.** I repeat this in the hope that even
> [critics] may understand."

and

> "**under no circumstances should orientations of buildings erected hundreds of years ago be
> investigated using MODERN data.** It is about 'them', not about 'us'."

[TIER A — King, "From Petra back to Mecca" (2017, abridged in *Suhayl* 2018) and "The Ottoman Mosque
Fallacy".]

**The palettes of accepted qiblas in three cities** [TIER A — King, citing a 12th-c. Andalusī
astrolabe treatise, al-Maqrīzī (d. 1442), and al-Bazdawī (d. 1089)]:

| City | Directions actually used, and why |
|---|---|
| **Córdoba** | 113° (standard approximate formula — "the error is more than 10°" for al-Andalus); 120° (winter sunrise); 135° (compromise E/S); **150° (the Great Mosque — parallel to the Kaaba's major axis)**; 180° (due south) |
| **Cairo** | 90° (due east); **117° (winter sunrise — *qiblat al-ṣaḥāba*)**; **127° (the astronomers', via Ibn Yūnus, 10th c.)**; 141° (Ibn Ṭūlūn); and anything in ca. 156°–204° (between Canopus's rising and setting) |
| **Samarqand** | **270° (due west — Ḥanafī, the road to Mecca)**; 240° (winter sunset, Great Mosque); 230°; 225° (Ḥanafī/Shāfiʿī compromise); **180° (due south — Shāfiʿī, the Prophet's qibla in Medina)** |

Note Samarqand: the Ḥanafī and Shāfiʿī qiblas there differ by **90°**, and both were accepted within
their schools. Any "deviation" figure computed against a modern azimuth for such a city is meaningless.

**The quantified medieval-vs-modern gap, with its cause.** Cairo:

> "**the modern qibla is some 8° south of the qibla of the medieval astronomers, because they relied
> on a value for the longitude difference which was too small by 3°.**"

[TIER A — King, in Rashed ed., vol. 1, ch. 4(a).]

**This relationship was verified by computation.** Formula:
`q = atan2(sin Δλ, cos φ·tan φ_K − sin φ·cos Δλ)`, Kaaba at 21.4225°N, 39.8262°E.

- Modern Cairo (30.0444°N, 31.2357°E) → azimuth **136.14°**, i.e. **46.1° south of east**. King says
  "about 45° South of East" [TIER A]. ✅ **Formula validated against a Tier A figure.**
- Sensitivity at Ghazna: **dq/dλ ≈ 1.05° of qibla per 1° of longitude error**; dq/dφ ≈ −1.8° per 1°
  of latitude error.

So a 3° longitude error → ~3° of qibla error directly; King's observed 8° gap at Cairo implies the
latitude and the formula choice contributed too. **The structure of the medieval error is: good
latitudes (a few arcminutes), bad longitudes (degrees), and that is the whole story.** Kennedy:
"Medieval latitude determinations were usually accurate to within a few minutes, but estimates of the
difference in longitude between Mecca and various localities **might be in error by several
degrees**"; "**By and large, the longitudes appearing in the texts are much less reliable than the
latitudes.**" [TIER A — King and Kennedy respectively, in Rashed ed., vol. 1.]

## 8. Synthesis: what would a 14th-century muwaqqit have made of ±12°?

### 8.1 The standards, kept separate

| Standard | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Theoretical ideal** (face the Kaaba's body) | **~0.0005°** (1–2 arcseconds) from Damascus/Cairo | computation, §4 |
| **Best achieved in the tradition** (al-Khalīlī's 2,880-entry table) | **±1–2 arcminutes = ±0.02–0.03°** | TIER A, King *JNES* 34 (1975) |
| **Competent practice** (King's summary) | **1–2°** | TIER A, King, repeatedly |
| **Best achieved field geodesy** (al-Bīrūnī's Δλ) | **~0.33°** error (Kennedy) / 2′ (Samsó) | TIER A, §3.2 |
| **Working instrument graduation** | **1°**, marked/labelled every 5° | TIER B + 6 objects, §2.1–2.2 |
| **What instruments actually DELIVERED** | **up to 11°** on a tabulated entry; some data "quite useless"; approximate formula **~10°** off | TIER A/C, King, §2.5 |
| **Actual Ottoman mosques, measured** | **6–18°** | TIER A, Ilçi et al. 2018 |
| **Juristic tolerance** (`jihat al-Kaʿba`) | **~90°** (al-Dimyāṭī, al-Maqrīzī's quarter-circle); some argued 180° | TIER A/B, §6.1–6.2 |

### 8.2 The direct answer

**By the standard of his own craft, a 14th-century muwaqqit would have regarded ±12° as poor — indeed
as not a determination at all.**

- He tabulated to **±1–2 arcminutes**. ±12° is **360–720× coarser** than his own verified table.
- His instruments were graduated in **whole degrees** — verified on six surviving objects plus a
  construction text. A ±12° uncertainty spans **24 graduations** of his compass rim; he could not even
  have *expressed* it as a reading, and would have called it a failure to find north rather than a
  qibla value.
- Al-Ashraf's error discipline was to eliminate a centring deviation visible "not even a little", and
  al-Bīrūnī's was three independent traverses to shave a third of a degree. Neither practice has room
  for 12°.

**But the honest comparison is against what instruments DELIVERED, not what they aspired to — and
there the gap narrows sharply.** King's own verdict on a 1°-graduated Isfahan compass is that its
maker was 11° out on Medina and favoured values rounding to 5°; on a 1°-graduated Damascus bowl, that
its data are "quite useless"; on the standard approximate formula, that it was ~10° off in al-Andalus
(§2.5). **So a 12° uncertainty is roughly the error a classical practitioner actually carried when
using a mediocre instrument or an approximate formula — while being two to three orders of magnitude
worse than the same tradition's best work.** Both halves are true, and the gap between them is the
gap between a muwaqqit's table and a jobbing instrument-maker's engraving.

**By the standard of the sacred law that same muwaqqit followed, ±12° is comfortably valid.**

- `jihat al-Kaʿba` admits ~**90°** (al-Dimyāṭī; al-Maqrīzī's "quarter of the horizon's circle"), and
  some argued the full 180°. ±12° is well inside either.
- **All four schools hold the prayer valid** when a diligent determination later proves mistaken
  (§6.3) — Hanbali and Hanafi outright, Maliki with a recommended repeat, Shāfiʿī split.
- The mosques he prayed in were themselves off by **6–18°** (Ilçi et al.), and the surveyors who
  measured them judged all of them "within acceptable boundaries".
- Nobody, ever, has faced the Kaaba's physical body from outside Mecca; it subtends ~1–2 arcseconds
  (§4). The jurists tracked this explicitly: the tolerance "**varies with nearness and farness**"
  (al-Rāfiʿī) and is "narrow in the extreme only for the people of the Sacred Mosque"
  (Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr).

**So the honest verdict is split, and the split is the finding:**

> ±12° would have been judged **a poor measurement but a sound prayer**. It fails the astronomer's
> craft standard by two to three orders of magnitude. It satisfies the jurist's validity standard
> with a factor of 7 to spare.

### 8.3 What this implies for a device threshold

Four load-bearing observations, each sourced:

1. **The tradition's own concept for "good enough" is *ijtihād*, and al-Ashraf applied it to an
   instrument by name.** His treatise determines the qibla **بحسب الاجتهاد والبحث** and closes
   "**we are *mujtahidūn*, not debaters**" [TIER B]. The precedent for a device is therefore not "be
   certain" but **"do the diligent thing and say honestly that it is approximate."**

2. **Classical practice was to disclose approximation verbally, not to withhold the reading.**
   Al-Ashraf's instrument gives a number, and every statement of it carries *bi-taqrīb* —
   "approximately" — covering declination, instrument deficiency, and operator error alike [TIER A —
   Schmidl]. He did not refuse to show north because the needle was imperfect; **he showed it and
   labelled it.** Direct precedent for showing a compass with a stated confidence rather than hiding
   it below a threshold.

3. **The sharpest juristic rule makes PROCESS decisive and OUTCOME insufficient.** Al-Nawawī: a
   *mujtahid* who does taqlīd has an invalid prayer **even if he hits the qibla** [TIER B —
   *al-Majmūʿ* 3/203–4]. And a *muqallid* is anyone ignorant of the evidences, **even a jurist** [Ibn
   Qudāma]. Applied to a device: **using the best determination the hardware can make is the
   obligation; being somewhat wrong having made it is forgiven.** Not using an available capability
   is the actual failure.

4. **Al-Juwaynī's criterion is the closest classical analogue to an uncertainty threshold, and it is
   expert-relative, not fixed.** The *jiha* is where "**the skilled person cannot say with certainty
   that facing has been lost**" [TIER B]. A device reporting its own uncertainty and drawing when that
   uncertainty is inside the acceptable span is implementing al-Juwaynī's test, not departing from it.

**A threshold in the 12° region sits in a defensible but unflattering place.** It is inside every
juristic tolerance attested (90°, and the 180° reading) and inside the measured spread of real Ottoman
mosques (6–18°). It is far outside what any classical *scientist* would have called a determination.
If the goal is "diligent effort honestly labelled", 12° passes. If the goal is "matches what the
tradition's experts achieved", nothing short of ~1–2° does — and King's "a degree or two" is the figure
to beat, because it is his repeated summary of what a competent medieval practitioner could do when he
tried.

**A caution against one tempting argument.** It is wrong to defend a loose threshold by pointing at
historical mosque deviations as if they were targets. King's whole corpus insists those deviations are
**mostly not errors at all** — they are different, deliberately chosen qiblas (folk-astronomical,
traditional, or computed from bad longitudes). Citing a 17° Ottoman mosque as licence for 17° of
sensor noise compares an intentional direction with an unintentional one. The defensible analogues for
*device uncertainty* are al-Ashraf's *bi-taqrīb* and al-Bīrūnī's averaging — the error-handling
practices — not the mosque orientations.

## 10. Calculations performed, with formulae

All computed independently; inputs and formulae given so they can be checked.

**Reconciling King's entry counts for al-Khalīlī's table.** Grid as King states it: latitudes 10°–56°,
longitude differences 1°–60°. Inclusive counts: 47 × 60 = **2,820**; 48 × 60 = **2,880**. King's
"2,880" is consistent with the grid; his "over 4,000" is not. → use 2,880. (§1.)

**Qibla azimuth (great circle, spherical Earth), Kaaba at φ_K = 21.4225°N, λ_K = 39.8262°E:**
```
Δλ = λ_K − λ
q  = atan2( sin Δλ,  cos φ · tan φ_K − sin φ · cos Δλ )
```
*Validation:* Cairo (30.0444°N, 31.2357°E) → q = 136.14° = 46.1° south of east. King states "about 45°
South of East" [TIER A]. Agreement within 1.1°, consistent with King rounding. ✅

**Sensitivity (Ghazna, 33.5531°N, 68.4228°E):** dq/dλ ≈ **+1.05°** of qibla per +1° of longitude;
dq/dφ ≈ **−1.8°** per +1° of latitude. Confirms the structure King describes: longitude errors of
"several degrees" produce qibla errors of several degrees.

**Great-circle distance (haversine), R = 6371.0088 km:**
```
a = sin²(Δφ/2) + cos φ₁ · cos φ₂ · sin²(Δλ/2);   d = 2R · arcsin(√a)
```

**Angular width subtended by the Kaaba, w = 12 m:**
```
θ = 2 · arctan( (w/2) / d )
```
→ 0.5″–1.9″ from the cities tabulated in §4.

**Lateral ground miss at Mecca for a heading error ε:**
```
miss = d · ε   (ε in radians)
```
→ 23–84 km per 1°; 270–1,004 km per 12°. (§4 table.)
